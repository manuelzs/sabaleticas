# Colombian Cattle Profitability — Research Briefing for Hacienda Sabaleticas

**Operation:** ~200 ha cattle fattening (*ceba*) in La Pintada, suroeste de Antioquia (tierra caliente, ~550–600 m, Cauca river valley). ~266 head, 96% female, model = *ceba de hembras* (buy thin females, fatten on pasture, sell on weight) plus some calves born on-farm from pregnant purchases, sold at weaning.
**Status:** Losing money monthly. **Purpose:** entry-point briefing on what profitable Colombian operations do differently.

**Glossary of Colombian terms used below:** *ceba* = fattening/finishing; *levante* = the growing/backgrounding phase (weaning → ~330 kg); *cría* = cow-calf breeding; *ciclo completo* = all three on one farm; *GDP / ganancia diaria de peso* = average daily gain (ADG), kg/animal/day; *potrero* = paddock; *aforo* = forage-mass / carrying-capacity estimate; *carga animal* = stocking rate; *UGG/UGM/UA* = "unidad de gran ganado" ≈ a 450–500 kg animal-equivalent; *en pie* = liveweight (price on the hoof); *flaco/a* = thin (store/feeder animal); *gordo/a* = finished/fat; *novillo* = steer; *novilla* = heifer; *vaca de descarte* = cull cow; *invierno* = rainy season, *verano* = dry season (note: in Colombia these refer to rain, not temperature); *rendimiento en canal* = carcass dressing %; *subasta/feria* = auction; *comisionista* = commission buyer/broker; *frigorífico* = slaughter plant.

> **Caveat on numbers:** Most hard cost/financial figures below come from AGROSAVIA's 2021 cost manual for the **Orinoquía** (Llanos/Meta), not Antioquia — labeled accordingly. Where a figure is from Brazil or another region it is flagged. Prices move fast; the 2024–2025 price points are time-stamped. Treat dated/regional figures as directional, not gospel. I did not invent any statistic; gaps are stated explicitly.

## Executive summary — 3–5 highest-leverage takeaways for THIS operation

1. **The price spread is the whole game, and it works against heavy cattle and against females.** In Colombia the $/kg *falls* as the animal gets heavier and is *lower* for females. As of mid-2025, macho de **levante** (lighter) averaged ~$10,013/kg while macho **gordo** (finished) averaged ~$9,021/kg — i.e. you get paid *less per kilo* for the very kilos you worked to add. Females sell at roughly a **10–15% discount** to males (hembra de primera ~$7,100–7,840/kg vs. macho gordo ~$8,000–9,000/kg in 2024–2025). A *ceba de hembras* therefore fights two headwinds at once. The margin must come from buying-spread + cheap kilos of gain, not from the sale price/kg.

2. **You are almost certainly under-stocked and under-gaining.** Colombia's *average* farm runs ~0.87 animals/ha and gains ~**350 g/day** with ~120–150 kg carne/ha/year and slaughter at ~40 months — these are the numbers of operations that *lose* money. Profitable tropical pasture operations target **2 animals/ha in invierno**, **0.6–0.85 kg/day GDP**, and **350–960+ kg/ha/year**. On ~200 ha, 266 head is light; the land can likely carry far more *if* pasture is fixed first.

3. **Pasture is the #1 lever and it's mostly free money.** Switching from continuous to **rotational grazing** (potreros divided, ~2–4 days occupation / 28–33 days rest) on improved *Brachiaria/Urochloa* raises GDP, carga, and kg/ha simultaneously. Silvopastoral systems (CIPAV/Fedegán GCS) push GDP from ~400 to **600–900 g/day** and carga toward **3–5 animals/ha**. This is the difference between the "tradicional" and "mejorada" ceba (12–14 vs 20–24 months to finish — see Table A).

4. **No scale, no báscula, no records = structural loss.** AGROSAVIA and Fedegán/CONtexto repeatedly identify the same failure modes: no weighing, "a memoria" management, free continuous grazing, low turnover, and selling to *comisionistas* at farm gate. Animal purchase (~41% of variable cost) + land rent (~25% of total cost) + labor (~18%) dominate the cost stack — so **rotation speed (turnover) and cost-per-kg-gained are what you must drive**, not gross sale price.

5. **Re-examine "ceba de hembras" itself.** It is *defensible* (females are cheaper to buy, more available, and with estrus control can finish acceptably) but it is the structurally lower-margin version of an already thin business. If the herd is 96% female and the farm is losing money, the first questions are pasture/carga/turnover — but a strategic question is whether the buy-cheap-female spread is actually large enough in *your* purchase channel to beat the ~10–15% sale discount.

## 1) The economics of ceba in Colombia — the price-spread trap

**How the fattening margin works.** Ceba profit = (kg sold × sale $/kg) − (kg bought × purchase $/kg) − cost of the kilos added − fixed/overhead. The naïve mental model is "buy at X $/kg, add weight, sell at X $/kg, pocket the gain × price." **That model is wrong in Colombia**, because:

- **$/kg compresses as the animal gets heavier.** Lighter feeder/levante animals trade at a *premium* per kilo; finished gordo trades at a *discount* per kilo. Mid-2025 national auction averages (CONtexto Ganadero, June 21–27 2025):
  - Macho de **levante**: ~$10,013/kg (Montería/Córdoba/Yopal $10,200–10,791)
  - Macho **gordo**: ~$9,021/kg (Medellín led at $10,308; Córdoba $10,100)
  - So a steer can be worth **~$1,000/kg LESS** as gordo than it was as levante. Every kilo you add is sold into a falling price.
- **This is the central trap:** the buyer pays top $/kg for the *frame* (the cheap-to-buy light animal someone else raised), and you add expensive kilos that are sold at the *lower* gordo price. Your margin is therefore the **buy/sell spread on the original frame** plus **the value of added kilos at the (lower) finished price minus what those kilos cost to put on**. If pasture is poor and the animal sits 18–24 months gaining 350 g/day, the cost of those kilos (land rent + time + finance) eats the entire spread.

**What GDP and days-on-feed make ceba profitable on tierra-caliente pasture.** From AGROSAVIA's Orinoquía comparison (Table A), the *mejorada* (profitable) model finishes in **12–14 months** vs. **20–24 months** for *tradicional*. Improved *Brachiaria* under rotation yields **15–20 kg/animal/month (≈0.5–0.65 kg/day) on pasture alone**; with restricted grazing + protein-energy supplement in the final phase, **1.0–1.3 kg/day** is reachable. The rule of thumb that emerges:

- **GDP below ~0.4 kg/day → losing money** (this is the national-average trap: 350 g/day, 40-month slaughter).
- **GDP ~0.5–0.7 kg/day on good rotated pasture → workable.**
- **GDP ~0.8–1.0+ kg/day with supplement/SSP in finishing → competitive.**
- **Days on feed:** the shorter the better — every extra month is land rent + finance + mortality risk burning the spread. Target finishing in **≤12–14 months**, not 20–24.

**Turnover (rotación de capital).** This is decisive and under-discussed. With a 12–14 month finishing cycle you turn the herd ~0.85–1×/year; at 20–24 months you turn it ~0.5×/year. The same land/labor/finance is spread over half as many sold animals — fixed cost per kg sold roughly *doubles*. Faster GDP → shorter cycle → more turns/year → fixed cost diluted. This is often the single biggest driver of whether a Colombian ceba makes or loses money.

**Mortality.** I did **not** find a clean, citable Colombian ceba-specific mortality benchmark in the sources reviewed; treat this as a gap to fill with farm records. (Industry rule-of-thumb for adult fattening cattle is low, ~1–3%/cycle, but I am flagging that I could not source a Colombian figure — do not quote it as fact.)

## 3) Pasture & forage — the biggest lever (best practices for tierra caliente Antioquia)

**Grasses for tropical lowland Antioquia (La Pintada elevation/climate).** The workhorses for hot lowland are:
- **Brachiaria/Urochloa**: *brizantha* (e.g. Toledo/Marandú), *decumbens*, and improved hybrids **Mulato II** and **Cayman** — the standard improved tropical grass; *Brachiaria* of good quality runs ~8% crude protein, 65% digestibility, ~1,000 kg DM/ha available; *B. brizantha* can yield ~5,000 kg DM/ha per grazing cycle with ~36-day rest.
- **Cynodon (estrella, *C. nlemfuensis*)**: high GDP — one study showed ~680 g/day vs. Mulato II ~500 g/day under tropical grazing.
- **Megathyrsus/Panicum maximum (guinea, Tanzania, Mombasa)**: high-yield, good in fertile hot lowland; used in SSP with Leucaena.

**Rotational grazing (pastoreo rotacional / Voisin).** This is the highest-ROI change available:
- Continuous grazing (one big undivided potrero, animals always on it) → overgrazing, selective grazing, "islas" of wasted forage, pasture degradation, <1 animal/ha, low GDP. **This is the default money-losing pattern and what most Colombian farms do.**
- Rotational: subdivide into potreros, **2–4 days occupation / 28–33 days rest** for Brachiaria; raises forage availability/quality, lets carga rise to **1/ha (verano) – 2/ha (invierno)**, and lifts GDP to **15–20 kg/animal/month**. Brachiaria forage production + leaf protein rose ~43% under rotational vs. traditional management (Amazon piedmont study).
- **Productivity jump:** intensified rotational Brachiaria reached **960–1,000 kg carne/ha/year vs. ~350 kg/ha** traditional — roughly **3×**.

**Aforo (forage-mass / carrying-capacity estimation).** Profitable operators run a *presupuesto forrajero* (forage budget): measure kg DM/ha available, divide by intake per animal, to set how many animals a potrero can hold and for how long. CONtexto and Fedegán stress this is the discipline most farms skip — they "introduce animals without any control into enormous areas." For Sabaleticas this means: measure pasture, set carga from data, don't eyeball it.

**Silvopastoral systems (SSP / SSPi — CIPAV, Fedegán "Ganadería Colombiana Sostenible").** Trees/shrubs (esp. **Leucaena leucocephala** at high density, 10,000+ plants/ha) intercropped with improved grass:
- GDP rises from ~**400 to 600–900 g/day** (UNAL/Montoya via CONtexto).
- Carga ~**3–5 animals/ha**; forage supply +27–63%; crude-protein intake +148%.
- Finishing in ~**26 months**; also shade (critical in tierra caliente — heat stress cuts intake/GDP), drought resilience, soil/erosion benefits, and carbon credits. Fedegán's GCS program (with CIPAV, TNC, Fondo Acción) is the institutional vehicle and may offer technical/financial support.
- *Caveat:* SSPi has higher establishment cost and a 1–2 year setup; it's the medium-term play, not the immediate fix. (Leucaena's standalone productivity figures weren't isolated in the sources.)

**Wet/dry season (invierno/verano) management.** Carga is seasonal: ~2/ha in invierno, ~1/ha in verano. Profitable farms bridge the dry season with **ensilaje (silage)** of cut grass/legumes/maize (a 65%-moisture silage gives ~350 g usable forage per kg) and finishing-phase supplement (0.5–2 kg concentrate/animal/day, ~$1,000/animal/day in 2018 pesos) to *hold carga and GDP through verano* instead of losing weight. La Pintada's Cauca-valley dry season is the period where unmanaged herds lose the year's gains.

## 4) Is "ceba de hembras" specifically a sound strategy?

**The honest answer: defensible but structurally lower-margin, and worth pressure-testing for THIS farm.**

- **Sale-price headwind (the main problem).** Females sell at a **discount** to males. 2024–2025 data: macho gordo national avg ~$8,000–9,021/kg; hembra de primera ~$7,115 (Nov 2024) to ~$7,843 (late 2024) /kg — roughly a **10–15% discount**. The 2026 projection shows fattened heifers ~$7,615→$7,817–8,999/kg. The market does discount females.
- **Performance headwind.** Females gain less and finish lighter: typical **400 g/day → 360 kg finished** vs. males **500 g/day → 480 kg**. Fewer kilos, slower, sold cheaper per kilo. (With good diet + estrus control, heifer ceba can be "as profitable as steers" per some vets, but the consensus for *pure meat production* leans male.)
- **Where ceba de hembras DOES make sense:** (a) females are **cheaper to buy and more available** — if your purchase channel gets females at a wide enough buy-spread, the cheaper entry can offset the cheaper exit; (b) cull cows / *vacas de descarte* feed the large regional "asadero" market — note **75% of Orinoquía regional consumption is cull cows and heifers for asaderos**, so there IS strong female demand, just at lower $/kg; (c) you avoid competing for scarcer, pricier males.
- **The strategic question for Sabaleticas:** with a 96%-female herd and monthly losses, is the female buy-spread in your actual purchase channel large enough to beat the 10–15% sale discount *and* the slower/lighter gain? If you can't quantify your buy-spread, you can't know if the female strategy is the loss source or just a contributor. **First fix pasture/carga/turnover (which dominate the math); then run the male-vs-female spread numbers on real purchase data before deciding to switch.**

**Selling calves at weaning vs. retaining.** On the on-farm calves from pregnant purchases: CONtexto/Fedegán note males require ~3 years to market and females ~4 years to first calving — i.e. retaining is a long capital lock-up. Selling at weaning frees capital fast but captures the *lowest* value point. The levante phase (weaning→330 kg) often has the **best $/kg** (recall macho levante ~$10,013/kg > gordo ~$9,021/kg in 2025) — so retaining calves through *levante* (not all the way to gordo) can be the higher-margin window, *if* pasture supports cheap gain. Given the farm's pasture is the binding constraint, selling at weaning is the lower-risk default until pasture/carga are fixed.

## 5) Why small/medium Colombian operations lose money — recurring failure modes & highest-leverage fixes

**Failure modes (consistent across AGROSAVIA, Fedegán, CONtexto):**
1. **Continuous free grazing, low carga** (~0.87/ha vs. 2/ha achievable) → overgrazing, degraded Brachiaria, selective grazing, wasted "islas," low GDP. *The #1 structural loss.*
2. **Low turnover** — 20–40 month cycles spread fixed cost over too few sales/year; monthly return collapses.
3. **No weighing / no records ("a memoria").** Most farms don't use the **báscula** as a management tool, only at sale. "Si no hay información, no hay nada." You can't manage GDP or cost-per-kg you don't measure.
4. **Adverse price spread mismanaged** — buying heavy or selling into the gordo $/kg discount without working the buy-spread; selling females without accounting for the discount.
5. **Subscale fixed costs** — land rent (25% of cost) and labor (18%) don't shrink with a half-empty pasture; under-stocking is doubly punishing.
6. **Informal selling at farm gate to comisionistas** — accepting the broker's price, no price discovery, no weighing scale, value captured by intermediaries (only "very few reach final markets").
7. **False economy on the cheap inputs** — cutting sanidad (<1% of cost) and nutrition (4%) starves the GDP that the whole margin depends on; not planning the dry-season ration → weight loss in verano.

**Highest-leverage fixes the literature/extension services recommend (in order of ROI):**
1. **Subdivide potreros and rotate** (occupation 2–4 d / rest 28–33 d). Low cash cost, immediate GDP + carga + kg/ha gain. Use *aforo*/forage budget to set carga from data.
2. **Buy and use a báscula; keep per-animal records** (entry weight, periodic weights every ~56 days, cost per animal). Manage to GDP and cost-per-kg-gained.
3. **Renovate/fertilize degraded pasture; plant improved Brachiaria/Cynodon**, then move toward **SSPi with Leucaena** (Fedegán GCS support) for shade + 600–900 g/day + 3–5/ha.
4. **Compress the cycle to ≤12–14 months** with finishing-phase supplement and dry-season silage; raise turnover.
5. **Push carga toward farm capacity** (fix pasture first) so the 200 ha and its fixed costs are fully used — likely well above 266 head once pasture is improved.
6. **Improve selling channel & price discovery** (see §6).
7. **Run real costos de producción** (AGROSAVIA's manual is a free template): track variable vs fixed, find where the money goes, compute c/b, VPN, TIR. Target **TIR > 12%**, **c/b > 1**.

## 6) Selling channels & price — discovery and timing

- **Channels (worst → best for price capture):**
  - **Comisionista / intermediario at farm gate** — most common, least price discovery; "ganaderos venden a intermediarios y muy pocos llegan a los mercados finales." Convenient but you take the broker's price, often without independent weighing.
  - **Subasta / feria ganadera (auction)** — transparent competitive price discovery; weighed; published averages let you benchmark. Regional auctions show meaningful spreads (e.g. mid-2025 macho gordo ranged $9,600 Caucasia → $10,308 Medellín). **Selling at a well-run subasta typically beats farm-gate.** Note: **Feria de Medellín consistently leads national gordo prices** — a relevant, nearby market for an Antioquia operation.
  - **Frigorífico / venta en canal (direct to plant)** — captures more of the chain (you also sell vísceras/subproductos), but requires volume, logistics, and knowing your animal's value along the chain; higher complexity.
- **Price discovery matters because the spread is wide.** Same week, same category, prices varied ~$1,000/kg across auctions/regions. Knowing published Fedegán/CONtexto price series and choosing where/when to sell is free margin.
- **Timing.** Cattle prices are cyclical and seasonal (retention/extraction cycles; dry-season supply swings). 2024–2025 saw a strong bull market (macho gordo ~$4,400 in 2019 → ~$8,000–9,000+ in 2024–2025, >100% in 5 years). Selling *finished* animals into strength, and avoiding forced dry-season sales of underweight cattle, protects margin. For a money-losing farm, **don't sell on the broker's schedule — sell weighed, into the best-discovered price, ideally at a strong auction like Medellín.**

## Gaps & honest caveats

- **No Antioquia-specific ceba cost study** was found; the hard cost/financial structure is AGROSAVIA's **Orinoquía** data (Piedemonte/Altillanura). Antioquia tierra caliente is climatically comparable, and 2017 cost/kg en pie was actually *lower* in Antioquia ($2,067) than Llanos ($2,767), so the economics may be somewhat *more* favorable — but the % cost structure and case TIRs are Orinoquía and should be re-derived from Sabaleticas' own books.
- **No clean Colombian ceba mortality benchmark** sourced — flagged, not invented.
- **Most price figures are 2024–2025 auction snapshots**; they will drift. The *relationships* (levante > gordo $/kg; male > female $/kg; ~3× kg/ha from rotation) are more durable than the absolute pesos.
- Some productivity figures (Leucaena+Brachiaria milk gains) are **Brazilian proxies**, labeled as such; the GDP/carga SSP figures are Colombian (CIPAV/UNAL/Fedegán).
- The AGROSAVIA case TIRs (36–59%) reflect specific 2018 assumptions and **good** management; they show ceba *can* be very profitable, not that it is by default. The same manual states the *national average* farm gains 350 g/day at 40 months — the losing baseline.
